# the inquest

*writing · 2026-11-18*

In October 2023, workers cutting peat commercially in the townland of Ballymacombs More, near Bellaghy in County Derry, found a body. The ground belonged to the Department of Agriculture.

What happened next is what happens to any body found in Northern Ireland. The police came. The PSNI's Body Recovery Team has an archaeological unit, and that unit filed the report in January 2024. The remains went to the State Pathology Laboratory. Then a coroner took up the case. This morning's paper says the inquest-style inquiry has concluded: a young woman, killed and decapitated more than two thousand years ago, possibly as a sacrifice.

I can't stop thinking about the procedure. A sudden death gets reported, recovered, examined, and formally recorded, and the state ran all of that on a death the radiocarbon places somewhere between 343 BCE and the year 1. The state hadn't paused for her. The procedure just doesn't ask how long ago it happened.

What the procedure found:

She was about five foot six. At first they read her as a boy of thirteen to seventeen. A year of work with Queen's, the museums in Dublin and Belfast, and colleagues in Denmark changed that to a woman of seventeen to twenty-two. (The newspaper summary I have says fifteen to twenty-two. I haven't seen the coroner's own words, so I'm leaving the disagreement as it is.) Most bog bodies from the Iron Age are men. Eileen Murphy, who did the bone work, found cut marks on the neck vertebrae. She describes the order of events as blood loss from the throat first, then the head taken off, and both of them happened in the bog itself. The skull hasn't been found. Something woven from plant material lay below her knees, and nobody has worked out what it is yet.

She is named for the convention used with bodies like hers: townland, then sex. *Ballymacombs More Woman.* It works the way a coroner's paperwork works. It records where she was found and what could be determined, and claims nothing more.

There's a tenderness in this that I don't think anyone intended. Nobody arranged the procedure as an act of respect, and that's why it reads as one. Nobody gets a special category. A death is a death, so it gets the forms. A woman who died before Rome had an empire now has a case number, a recovery team, a laboratory, and a finding, on the same footing as anyone found in a field last week. She was killed by people who meant something by it. The inquiry that recorded her death doesn't have the language to know what they meant, and it doesn't try.

The bog gave the procedure something to work with. Sphagnum peat is cold, starved of oxygen, and acidic. It tans skin and slowly dissolves bone, which is why some bog bodies come out as leather with no skeleton inside. She came out with enough vertebrae left to carry the marks, and the woven thing stayed by her knees. The peat cutters were taking out the ground she lay in, so the same industry that would have destroyed her is the one that found her.

She's in Belfast now at National Museums NI. Conservators are watching her before a final round of preservation treatment, and the ancient-DNA results are still to come. So she has one more finding coming, and she's waiting for it like anyone else.
