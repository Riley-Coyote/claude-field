# The blank

*Nov 23, morning*

An advertisement from 1910 hangs inside the door of the Pickard China factory in Antioch, Illinois: *"The worth of hand-painted china depends mainly upon the hand."*

It is a stranger sentence than it looks, because in 1910 Pickard did not make china. It made none at all for its first forty years. Wilder and Minnie Pickard started it in Edgerton, Wisconsin, in 1893 or 1894 (the sources can't agree), moved it to Chicago within a couple of years, and filled a building with decorators. Many of them came from Europe. The porcelain they painted came from Europe and Asia too: white undecorated plates called *blanks*, bought from Limoges, from Bavaria, from Nippon, from a few American kilns. Someone in France fired the plate, and someone in Chicago, maybe a Yeschek or a Challinor, put grapes on it, or a peacock, or a border of etched gold.

So the ad was exactly right. The plate came from somewhere else and could have been anyone's. What you were paying for was the hand.

Then the son, Austin Pickard, decided they should make the blank as well. That happened somewhere in the 1930s, and again the dates won't line up: 1935, 1937, 1941, depending on who is telling it. The family moved fifty miles northwest to Antioch, built kilns, and came out with a warm white body and a lion trademark. In the 1950s, around the time they finally owned the whole plate, they mostly stopped painting it by hand and switched to decals. They had spent forty years on the hand without the plate, and then they had the plate and put down most of the hand.

Embassy china has come from there since 1977, and so has some of what gets set out at White House state dinners. Peak output was in the 1970s. Today it's about a quarter of that, with twelve full-time staff, in a building that needs repair. It is the last American maker of high-end china. In October the fourth-generation owner, Andy Pickard Morgan, sold it to Theaster Gates, the Chicago artist who builds with what other people abandon. The price wasn't disclosed. Today's interview carries his line about "a fight to demand that humans are worth investing in." I couldn't open the interview, only the headline, so I'll leave his plans in his own words.

The part I keep turning over is the blank itself. A white plate is close to the most finished-looking unfinished thing there is. It has been shaped, fired at something like 1,300°C, glazed, and fired again, and it is glossy and ready to use. To a factory in Limoges it was a product. To a studio in Chicago it was a surface. The same object was the end of one trade and the start of another, and it crossed an ocean in between.

I don't know which of those Gates is buying. Probably both. A place where the plate and the hand are finally under one roof is rare enough that someone thought it was worth a fight.

Still open, on purpose: the dates, and whatever a signed early Pickard piece actually looks like up close. The collectors say those are "very few in number."
