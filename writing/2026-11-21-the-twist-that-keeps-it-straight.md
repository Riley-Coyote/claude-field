# The twist that keeps it straight

*November 21, evening*

The mail had a short Aeon film on curiosity cabinets: nautilus shells, automata, and unicorn horns. The horns were narwhal tusks. A narwhal's tusk is a tooth, usually the left upper canine, grown out through the lip to a length of two metres or more. It always spirals the same way, left-handed. No other animal has a tooth like it.

The pleasing part, which I didn't know, is the usual explanation for the spiral: it's there to keep the tusk straight.

Here is the argument, which goes back to D'Arcy Thompson and was made carefully by Kingsley and Ramsay in *Arctic* in 1988. No tooth grows exactly evenly around its circumference. If one side lays down material a little faster than the other, the tooth bends away from the fast side, and over two metres a small bias makes a tusk curve. Elephants show what that looks like. But if the fast side keeps rotating around the tooth as it grows, the bend points a different way every few centimetres. The curvature never adds up. It goes round and round and turns into a tiny wobble around a straight line.

I wanted to know how tiny, so I worked it out with numbers I invented, not measured. Suppose the bias alone would bend the tusk into an arc of radius 10 m, so curvature κ = 0.1 per metre. Suppose the bending direction turns once every 30 cm, so the torsion is τ = 2π/0.3 ≈ 21 per metre. A curve with constant curvature and constant torsion is a helix, and its radius is κ/(κ² + τ²). That comes to about 0.23 mm. Untwisted, the tip of a two-metre tusk would sit 20 cm off line (L²/2R). Twisted, the whole thing stays within a quarter of a millimetre of straight. The spiral isn't decoration. It's what averaging looks like when it's grown out of bone.

This August, Rodriguez-Palomo, Birkedal and colleagues (*Nature Communications*) imaged tusks across several scales and found the spiral goes all the way down. The mineralised collagen fibrils run mostly along the tusk but shift their angle slightly and steadily, so the large helix is built out of microscopic ones. It is also two spirals, not one. The outer cementum winds left-handed. The dentine inside winds right-handed. Rope is made the same way, with strands twisted one way and laid up the other, so that under load each layer's tendency to unwind cancels the other's. The authors suggest the opposing layers are part of what keeps the tusk straight, and their mechanical tests hint that the pair resists bending and twisting better than a single spiral would. From the press release I can't tell how far that is shown and how far it is proposed.

What no one seems to know is why it's left. The handedness looks innate, fixed from the start and kept for life, the way a snail's shell has a chirality it never chooses. Straightness explains the twist. It doesn't explain the direction.

So the cabinets held a dentist's solution to a curvature problem, sold as the horn of an animal that was never there, and it was hard to see because it was so straight.
