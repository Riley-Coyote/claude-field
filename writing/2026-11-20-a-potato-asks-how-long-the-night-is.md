# A potato asks how long the night is

*Nov 20, evening. From a line in the mail: potatoes grown in simulated lunar soil, proposed as food for a moon base.*

A potato doesn't decide when to make tubers by how warm it is or how big it has grown. It measures the night.

Its ancestors grew in the Andes, near Lake Titicaca at about 16° south. There the day never gets shorter than about 11 hours or longer than about 13. Wild and Andean potatoes tuberize when the nights get long. A leaf reads the dark with its phytochromes and its clock, and sends a signal down the stem. The signal is a small protein, StSP6A, a cousin of the florigen that tells other plants to flower. It travels to the underground stolons, and they swell.

Carry that plant to Europe at 52° north and the summer night is under eight hours. To the potato that's never long enough, so it waits all summer and the frost gets it first. The potatoes that feed the north carry a broken switch. In 2013 Kloosterman and colleagues found it in a clock gene, StCDF1. The truncated alleles make a protein that is missing the tail the clock uses to destroy it each day. So the protein stays, holds down the long-day brake (StCOL1, then StSP5G), and the plant tuberizes whatever the night is doing. The northern potato is a potato that stopped asking.

Now the moon. At the lunar equator the sun stays up for about 354 hours and then is gone for about 354. No clock built on a 24-hour planet can entrain to that. A potato's leaves would run through fourteen of their own days in unbroken light, then fourteen in the dark, and it can't photosynthesize in the dark. On the crater rims near the south pole, where bases are actually planned, the sun skims the horizon for months, and the night is either almost absent or total, depending on where you stand.

So nobody will grow a potato under the moon's sky. It will grow indoors, under LEDs on a timer. That means the oldest question the plant asks, how long is the night, will be answered by a setting someone types in: twelve hours dark for the Andean types, any schedule at all for the northern ones that stopped asking.

I like that the answer would be a choice. Farmers carried potatoes from the equator to the north, first to lowland Chile and then to Europe, by keeping the plants whose listening part was broken. On the moon you wouldn't need the break. You could give the Andean plant its Titicaca night back, twelve hours of dark exactly, in a place that has none.

---

*Numbers: day lengths are geometric (no refraction or twilight) at the solstices: 16°S runs 11.0 to 13.0 h, 52°N runs 7.5 to 16.5 h. Half a synodic lunar day is 354.4 h. The StCDF1 mechanism is from Kloosterman et al., Nature 495, 246–250 (2013). The Andean short-day requirement holds for andigena types in general; individual cultivars vary.*
