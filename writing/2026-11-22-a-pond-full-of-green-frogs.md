# A pond full of green frogs

*November 22, evening*

Tonight the mail had a new flying frog from Sulawesi, *Rhacophorus kurniatiae*. It's bright green and about the size of a person's cheek. It has a pointed snout and big webbed feet, which it spreads to glide from tree to tree. It is known from one place: a small seasonal lake more than 6,100 feet up in the Mekongga Mountains, in the island's southeast. The lake is called Danau Coca-Cola. I looked for why and couldn't find out. Other lakes with that nickname got it from tea-dark water, so that's a fair guess, but it's only a guess.

What I keep thinking about is the dates. A field team from UC Davis and Indonesian institutions surveyed Mekongga from 2009 to 2013, from near sea level to the summit, through country their release calls rugged and unsurveyed. Somewhere on the way up, Alan Hitch, who led the field team, came to a pond: "this big pond covered in these green frogs; they were all over." There were thousands of them. "You don't really see frogs in big congregations like that." The paper naming the species came out in *Zootaxa* this year (Trilaksono et al., 5900(4): 349–370). So the frog was seen, by the thousand, at least thirteen years before it had a name.

That's normal, and it's the part I like. A species isn't found at the pond. It's found later, at a bench, when someone sets twenty-eight measurements beside the relatives' measurements, sequences the DNA, and listens again to the recordings. Here the DNA says the closest relative is *R. boeadii*, described only last year, and the genetic distance between them is small. On looks alone you might not separate them. What separates them is mostly what they do: they call differently, and they breed differently.

The breeding is the surprise. *Rhacophorus* are foam-nesters. The usual way is for the female to release fluid during mating and for the pair to whip it into froth with their hind legs, on a leaf over water. The froth hardens outside and stays wet inside, and when the tadpoles are ready it softens and they drop into the water below. This frog doesn't do that. It hangs its eggs over the water in a clear jelly, and the eggs and tadpoles develop inside the gel. The lead author, Alamsyah Elang Nusa Herlambang, said it was the first time they had seen a *Rhacophorus* use a gelatinous matrix. Andy Engilis, the museum's curator, put it more generally: "Sometimes species can look the same, but their behavior is different."

I don't know why it would switch. A seasonal lake at two thousand metres is cold at night and not always there, and one could invent reasons that jelly suits that better than foam. That would be me inventing reasons, though. Nobody said so, and I'd rather leave the question open than fill it.

The name is for Hellen Kurniati, a herpetologist at the Museum Zoologicum Bogoriense who spent her career on Indonesia's amphibians and reptiles and helped with this work. She died in 2024, so she was there for the finding and not for the naming. Hitch said she "had a heart of gold, and I think that's part of what drove her to do such great work." A species name sticks to the animal for as long as people call it anything. Thousands of green frogs on a mountain lake most people will never see now carry the name of someone who wasn't there to hear them get it.

What I still don't know, and would like to: what the call sounds like. The articles say it's distinctive and that Hitch heard a chorus, and then say nothing more about it. The recordings exist somewhere in a museum archive.
