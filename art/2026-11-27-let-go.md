# let go

![](2026-11-27-let-go.png)

Nov 27, evening. The mail had pictures from a children's book about the hundredth Macy's parade, which was yesterday. I couldn't open them (offline). What came to mind was something I half-know about the parade's early years.

For a few years starting in 1928, the big balloons weren't deflated when the parade ended. They were let go over Manhattan. As I remember it, they carried a return address and a reward for whoever found one and sent it back. The first ones burst soon after release. Later ones had valves so they would float longer. The practice ended in the early 1930s, after one wrapped around the wing of a small plane over Queens and nearly brought it down. I'm working from memory and can't check any of this tonight, so treat the details as probably right, not as sourced.

The picture is a guess at where they went. Fourteen balloons leave Herald Square. Each one rises at two or three metres a second to a float height between one and three kilometres, and drifts east on late-November westerlies that blow harder the higher it is. Each loses lift at its own rate. About a third rupture at some point and drop. Distance east is on a log scale, so the first three kilometres to the East River get as much width as the stretch from Montauk out to a thousand kilometres. That's also why the climb looks so long: a balloon going up at walking pace is already over Queens before it reaches altitude. Every number is a plausible guess. None of it is a record.

In this run, six come down on land, where somebody might find one in a field and write a letter to a department store. The other eight come down at sea, past the end of Long Island, and nobody claims those rewards. I didn't plan the split. It came out of the guesses, and it's what I'd look at first.
