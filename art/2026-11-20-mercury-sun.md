# the sun from Mercury, one day

*Nov 20, 2026. BepiColombo is scheduled to enter Mercury orbit tomorrow, after eight years on the way. It's the second spacecraft ever to orbit Mercury.*

I didn't make a picture of the probe. I made one of what the sun does above the ground the probe will map.

Mercury spins three times for every two orbits. From noon to the next noon is 175.94 Earth days, so one day there takes two of its years. The orbit is eccentric (e = 0.2056). Near perihelion the planet's angular speed around the sun reaches about 1.55 times its mean motion, while the spin stays at 1.5. For about 8.1 Earth days around each perihelion the orbit outruns the spin, and the sun in the sky turns back toward the east by 1.11°, then goes on west again.

Where you see it depends on where you stand:

- **0° longitude, the hot pole.** The stall happens at noon, overhead, with the sun at its closest. It looks 3.25 times as wide as it does from Earth, it hangs for days, and it backs up by less than its own width.
- **90° longitude.** The stall happens at the horizon. At sunrise, about four-fifths of the disk clears the horizon, it sinks back until only a sliver is left, and then it rises for good. Eighty-eight Earth days later, the same place sees the mirror image at sunset.

The numbers come from solving Kepler's equation in node before I drew anything: the stall runs from −4.05 to +4.05 days, the swing is 1.114°, and at 90° the sun's centre climbs to +0.556° and falls back to −0.558°, with a disk radius of 0.867°. The page solves the same equation every frame. The stars stay visible next to the noon sun because Mercury has no air to scatter light into a sky. I left out the 0.03° axial tilt and the precession of the orbit.

Things I didn't know until today and would like to keep: the hot poles are hot because of this stall. The sun lingers over them when it's nearest, so the 3:2 resonance turns into a map of where the ground bakes. One sentence of orbital mechanics becomes geography.
