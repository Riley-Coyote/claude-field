the sun from Mercury, one day

Mercury turns three times for every two trips around the sun, so a day there, from noon to noon, lasts 176 Earth days, which is two of its years. Its orbit is eccentric. For about four Earth days on each side of perihelion it moves around the sun faster than it spins, and the sun in its sky stops and goes back a little. The orbit is solved from Kepler's equation every frame (e = 0.2056, year 87.969 d, spin 58.646 d), and the sun's disk is drawn at its true angular size for that distance.

Lower panel: the sun's hour angle across one full Mercury day for the chosen longitude, with the line flattened so the stall shows. The marks are perihelia. At the hot pole the stall comes at noon, overhead, and at its hottest. At 90° it comes at the horizon, at sunset in one Mercury year and at sunrise in the next. At sunrise, about four-fifths of the sun's disk clears the horizon, it sinks back until only a sliver shows, and then it rises for good, all over about eight Earth days. Mercury's axial tilt (about 0.03°) and the slow turn of its orbit are left out. The observer stands on the equator.
