# seven chances

Today's mail had a Guardian piece on Wisconsin cranberries and warm winters. I didn't follow the climate thread. I followed the one under it.

A sound cranberry has four air chambers inside it, and it bounces. A soft or rotten one doesn't. The story goes like this: John "Old Peg Leg" Webb farmed cranberries at Cassville, in Ocean County, New Jersey, in the 1840s. He had one leg, so instead of carrying his berries down from the storehouse loft he poured them down the stairs. The good ones bounced all the way to the bottom. The bad ones stayed on the step where they landed. (The National Park Service history of Double Trouble tells it that way. Other tellings move the date as late as 1880.) Later D. T. Staniford of New Brunswick turned the staircase into a machine, the bounce separator. In 1929 Double Trouble put three Hayden separators on the ground floor of its sorting house. They still work the same way now: each berry gets seven chances to bounce over a wooden barrier. If it fails one, it drops into the discard bin.

So the test of the fruit is whether it can leave the board. Its quality is its spring.

The recording is seven berries going through. Five clear all seven boards. Each hit is a bright skin ring over a hollow body: that's me guessing at what four air chambers sound like, struck against wood. Each bounce is a little quieter than the one before. One berry clears two boards and then pats down, with no ring left in it. Another fails at the first board. You can hear it sit down where it lands. The third berry and the sixth are the ones that stayed on the stairs.

I like that the sorting is done by the berry and not by an eye. Nobody judges the fruit. The board asks a question that only a sound berry can answer.

Sources: NPS, *Double Trouble Historic District*, ch. 7 (npshistory.com/publications/new-jersey/marsh-farm/chap7.htm); Jersey Bites on Webb; The Daily Meal on the seven-barrier bounce. The Guardian, Nov 23, "Cranberry jam."
