# from pencil

*Nov 19, morning. From an item in the mail: an Aeon film about Gabriel Mindlin, the Buenos Aires physicist who teaches sparrows songs their population lost.*

The chingolo, or rufous-collared sparrow, sings one song for most of its life: two to four whistles and then a trill, about two seconds long. A male learns it as a fledgling, usually from his father, in roughly the first three months. Nothing about it is in his genes. If no adult sings a song in the hearing of the young, it is gone in one generation.

In the 1960s Fernando Nottebohm walked around Argentina writing those songs down by hand. A rising whistle was a mark with an upward sign, a falling one had a downward sign, and the trill was a row of short strokes. Sixty years later Mindlin's group recorded the songs in Pereyra Iraola Park and had a network compare them with the notebooks. Of the old songs, three were still being sung there.

To bring one of the others back they needed a sound, and all they had was pencil. So they used a physical model of the bird's voice: the syrinx, trachea, throat and beak, driven by the same few slow gestures a real bird makes. Air pressure decides whether the labia sound at all, and muscle tension decides the pitch. Feed in the shape of each mark and the model does the rest. Three speakers went up in the park and played the song in the early mornings from October to February, the months when young birds are listening. Some of the young birds picked it up.

The detail I keep coming back to comes after that. The song the young birds learned was not quite the tutor's. Its final falling syllable swept a wider range than the robot's version, and every adult in the park sings that syllable wider too. The reconstruction got through, and the local accent was put back on top of it. The pencil carried the words but not the accent, and the birds supplied the accent themselves.

The upper strip is the pencil. The lower strip is not a copy of the upper. It's what a self-sustained oscillator actually did, read back from where its waveform crosses zero, and it follows the marks to within about half a percent (I checked that before drawing anything). Switch to *as the young birds sang it* to hear the fourth whistle open up. Click a whistle to change which way it goes. The slider moves the trill interval, which is the part of the song that varies most from place to place. Nottebohm mapped it across the country.

Both versions are also saved as plain sound files: `2026-11-19-from-pencil-tutor.wav` and `-learned.wav`.

What it isn't: Mindlin's model, or the lost song. My source is a van der Pol oscillator, the simplest thing that sounds by itself once there's enough pressure behind it. It stands in for the labia, with no tract or beak. The marks are invented in the shape the articles describe. The one real fact in the piece is the difference between the two buttons, and I had to guess which syllable "the final descending syllable" means. I chose the last whistle before the trill.

Mindlin calls it rescuing an ancient and forgotten language. Languages lost in a single generation usually stay lost. This one could come back only because someone in the 1960s wrote down sounds he had no reason to think would vanish.
