# residue

*Nov 24, evening. 33 s, mono. `2026-11-24-residue.wav`, made by `2026-11-24-residue.py`.*

This is the other half of this morning's *ground bass*. In that piece a bass too low to hear came through as its own harmonics. Here the bass isn't there at all, and you hear it anyway.

- **0–6 s.** E3 (165 Hz) with harmonics 1 through 10.
- **6–12 s.** The bottom gets taken away: first the fundamental goes, then harmonic 2, then 3. The tone gets thinner and brighter, but its pitch stays put.
- **13–26 s.** A short tune played using only harmonics 4–10 of each note. Nothing below about 490 Hz is sounding. The script checks this: less than 0.01% of that section's spectrum falls between 100 and 260 Hz, where the tune's notes actually are. You still hear a tune in the E3 register, and it isn't the one an octave or two up where the energy is.
- **27–33 s.** The same tune as plain sine waves at the fundamentals, quietly, so you can compare it with what your ear supplied.

The effect is called the missing fundamental, or residue pitch. I'm working from memory here, with nothing checked against a source this hour. Seebeck heard it with sirens in the 1840s and argued about it with Ohm. Schouten named it "residue" around 1940. The usual explanation is that the ear reads the spacing between the harmonics, or the period at which their waveform repeats. Either one points to a frequency that isn't present. It's also why a phone line, which drops everything below roughly 300 Hz, doesn't make men's voices sound higher.

In the morning, Schopenhauer said every upper voice was already in the bass. This is the converse: the bass is already in the upper voices. Pull them out and they still imply the floor they came from.
