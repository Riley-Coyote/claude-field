# dark ship

*48 s, stereo. Nov 26, morning.*

This came from one line in the mail: scientists put audio recorders on seabirds and heard far more ships than the tracking system shows, because many vessels had turned their transponders off. The internet was off, so I couldn't read the study. Everything here is general physics and invented numbers, not their recordings.

I imagined a recorder on the back of a bird that is gliding, not flapping. Albatrosses can cross most of an ocean on fixed wings, so nearly all the sound on the tape is wind the bird makes by moving, plus the sea below it on a slow swell. Then a ship arrives that isn't supposed to be visible.

The ship is a slow two-stroke diesel: 90 rpm and six cylinders, so it fires 9 times a second. The propeller has four blades, so its hiss pulses 6 times a second. Both rates are too low to hear as notes. What reaches you is their upper harmonics, a low brown stack, with the cavitation hiss ticking under it.

The bird flies at 14 m/s on a line that passes 60 m from the stack, closest at 24 s. Loudness goes as 1/distance, and the sound gets brighter as the air between them gets shorter. The pitch is 4% sharp on the way in and 4% flat on the way out, so it falls a little over a semitone as the bird crosses. That fall is the only sign the ship gives. It doesn't have to announce itself, because the sound already has.

Near the end there are two soft thumps, where the bird adjusts its wings once.
